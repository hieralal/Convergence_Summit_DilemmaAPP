Tab 01 · The dilemma

Three forces, one budget, one accountable leader

This app is a working instrument for the dilemma at the heart of the United Leaders Convergence Summit: organisations are asked to create more value and move faster, while remaining resilient and in control — with the same money, the same people and the same legacy.

Can organisations create more value, move faster and remain competitive — without losing resilience, control or the freedom to adapt?
Force 1

Value

Growth, measurable productivity and genuine reinvention — not another tool layered on existing work.

Force 2

Speed

Time-to-value, competitive clock speed and the ability to adopt AI before the window closes.

Force 3

Control

Resilience, regulatory compliance, data protection and sovereignty over critical dependencies.

Why it is a dilemma, not a trade-off

A trade-off has a right answer once you know the constraints. This dilemma does not: the three forces are funded from the same envelope, owned by different executives with different accountability, and measured on different clocks. The panel's premise is that the answer is architectural — deciding deliberately where speed belongs, where control is non-negotiable, and where value is actually created — rather than financial, where the loudest voice wins.

  • Private vs public: universities and public institutions carry continuity of essential services and societal dependencies that shape what is possible.
  • Inside vs outside: disruption increasingly starts beyond the enterprise boundary — third parties, infrastructure, geopolitics.
  • Now vs later: resilience investment pays off in a disruption that may never occur.

About this app

This interactive briefing was created for the United Leaders Convergence Summit (12 November 2026, Amsterdam) as a companion to the cross-functional executive panel "The New Enterprise Equation". It was created by Dennis Hieralal (Lead Architect, Erasmus University) in cooperation with Dennis Torrepalma (United Leaders).

The app is deliberately not a slide deck. It is a thinking instrument: each tab holds one perspective on the same dilemma, so the conversation can move between them without losing the thread. Nothing here is confidential — all examples are generic or public.

How to use it

Four moves

  • Start here — frame the dilemma, not the solution
  • Align — put the 4 dimensions against the 3 equations
  • Step into a seat — read one perspective at a time
  • Close — one decision for the next 90 days
Tab 07 · Self-assessment

Where does your own organisation stand?

The last tab turns the equation into a quick diagnostic. Four disciplines — Technology, Business, Finance and Risk — each get five simple questions, answered on a three-point scale (Not yet · Partly · In place). It takes about five minutes.

As you answer, the app calculates a maturity score per discipline (0–100%) and an overall maturity score with a band — Exposed, Emerging, Developing or Leading. It then points you to where to start: the lowest-scoring discipline, which is most likely the constraint on the whole equation, plus the first concrete move and the strength to build on.

It is a conversation instrument, not an audit — its value is in showing where the four seats rate their own organisation differently, and in giving the panel a shared starting point for the 90-day decision.

The three equations

Three questions, each asking for something different from the same four dimensions.

I

Transformation

Where do we accelerate?

Legacy complexity, regulatory obligations and limited resources slow the organisation down. The question is where speed is worth buying — and where it is not.

Test: does the change remove a dependency, or add one?

II

Resilience

What happens when it fails?

Even when your own IT is running, the systems you depend on can fail — identity, cloud regions, network, payments. Disruption increasingly starts outside the boundary.

Test: would our dashboard stay green while the business is down?

Leadership

When value, speed and control point in different directions, someone must be accountable for the residual risk — and everyone must know who that is.

Perspective 1 · CIO / Digitalisation

"Where do we accelerate — and what do we simplify first?"

The CIO owns the transformation agenda and the portfolio. The dilemma lands here first: every acceleration request and every control request draws on the same delivery capacity.

Signature question · I

When is architectural redesign essential, rather than adding another technology layer?

Transformation that only adds tools automates existing work. The test: does the change remove a dependency, or add one?

Signature question · II

Which external failures would stop us, even if our own IT is running?

Identity providers, cloud regions, network, payment, logistics, public transport — the CIO's own dashboard is green while the business is down.

Signature question · III

What is our one transformation or simplification priority?

Not a list. One. The thing that, if done, makes the rest easier.

The CIO's tension

Portfolio gravity

Legacy, compliance and resilience consume the majority of budget and capacity; innovation gets what is left. Without deliberate portfolio choices, the "transformation" line item slowly becomes maintenance.

What good looks like

  • A named portfolio split (optimise / transform / reinvent) that leadership has agreed to, not a wish list
  • Reuse before buy before build as an operational rule, not a principle on a poster
  • A dependency map that includes parties outside the enterprise
  • One simplification per year that actually removes something
Practical examples

Patterns seen in practice

  • Application rationalisation: kill the quick wins, project-manage the rest
  • AI as a capability under governance, not a shadow channel per department
  • Central software catalogue: reuse → buy → build
Perspective 2 · CISO / Security

"What must never fail — and who may say stop?"

Security is asked to enable speed and guarantee control at the same time. The honest answer is that it can do the first only if leadership accepts a defined risk appetite for the second.

Signature question · I

How does the duty to protect data change the speed and priorities of transformation?

Protection is not a brake on transformation; it is a constraint on the sequence. The question is who decides the sequence.

Signature question · II

Which services must be restored first — and how do we coordinate with others?

Disruption by design, not for profit, changes the threat model: availability and integrity come before confidentiality.

Signature question · III

Should security have authority to stop a programme?

Or challenge and escalate while leadership retains accountability? Both are defensible; ambiguity is not.

The CISO's tension

Control that adds complexity

Every control has a cost in friction, exceptions and shadow work. Controls that cannot show a risk reduction become the reason people route around them.

What good looks like

  • A written risk appetite that finance and the board have signed, not a security document
  • Escalation with a clock: challenge, decide, record — no silent vetoes
  • Resilience tested against third-party and public-infrastructure failure, not only own-IT outages
  • Controls with a sunset date unless they demonstrably reduce risk
Practical examples

Patterns seen in practice

  • Regulation as competitive variable: compliance as a differentiator, not only a cost
  • Privileged access: policy that people can actually follow beats policy that they work around
  • Graceful degradation designed in advance, not improvised during an incident
Perspective 3 · Finance

"How do we fund both innovation and preparedness?"

Finance is asked to compare a certain cost today with an uncertain avoidance tomorrow. The dilemma is not whether resilience has value, but how to account for value that shows up as something that did not happen.

Signature question · I

How should we prioritise when innovation, compliance and resilience compete?

Three different clocks: quarterly value, annual assurance, multi-year preparedness. One budget cycle cannot hold all three without explicit rules.

Signature question · II

How do we evaluate preparedness for interruptions from outside the organisation?

Expected loss, tolerance thresholds and insurance logic — applied to technology dependencies as well as assets.

Signature question · III

What is our one financial discipline?

A rule that makes the balance between innovation and risk explicit, and repeatable.

Finance's tension

The invisible benefit

Resilience is only visible when it is missing. That asymmetry structurally disadvantages preparedness in any budget contest decided on measurable return.

What good looks like

  • A separate resilience envelope, not a line item that competes with growth
  • Stage-gated funding: small bets released on evidence, not annual big-bang approvals
  • Dependency concentration as a risk metric with a threshold (e.g. no single provider above X% of critical capability)
  • Kill criteria agreed before the investment starts
Practical examples

Patterns seen in practice

  • Portfolio funding with a fixed share for reinvention, protected from reallocation
  • Sovereignty premium expressed as cost per unit of strategic risk removed
  • Total cost of dependency, not only licence cost
Perspective 4 · Architecture

"Can architecture be the third option?"

Between "move fast" and "no" there is a third answer: redesign the dependency so the trade-off disappears. Architecture is the discipline that turns a dilemma into a design question.

Architecture is the place where value, speed and control are not played off against each other, but deliberately distributed. Not everything needs to be fast. Not everything needs to be controlled. But you must know what belongs where — and that is an architectural choice, not a budget choice.
Signature question · I

When is architectural redesign essential instead of another layer?

Another layer buys time and adds coupling. Redesign removes the constraint. The question is which of the two you are paying for.

Signature question · II

How do we design for disruption across interconnected systems?

Graceful degradation, bounded blast radius, substitutable components and explicit dependency contracts.

Signature question · III

Which architecture principle or critical dependency do we address in 90 days?

One principle. One dependency. Chosen because it unlocks the most other decisions.

Architecture's tension

Sovereignty is a property of the architecture, not of the contract

A contract that says "you may switch" is worthless if the data, identities, logs and knowledge make switching practically impossible. Exit is an architectural feature.

What good looks like

  • Decision-ready architecture: options with costs, risks and exit paths — not diagrams
  • Explicit dependency register with criticality, substitutability and switch cost
  • Separation of concerns: infrastructure, application and data layers with their own sovereignty and speed profiles
  • Architecture involved at the start, with a mandate — not at the end, as a review gate
Practical examples

Patterns seen in practice

  • AI: a sovereign model layer as a third layer next to the productivity channel and own infrastructure — complementary, not competing
  • Rationalisation: one model (capabilities, applications, contracts) instead of four spreadsheets
  • Compliance translated into architecture principles, so it is designed in rather than audited on
Closing · 90 days

The third option in one sentence

"We will not choose between speed and control — we will redesign the one dependency that forces the choice, and we will know within 90 days whether it worked."
